:::key-concept
Trust signals are the evidence that a real, qualified person stands behind your site. Google's quality guidelines call this E-E-A-T: experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust, and trust matters most. For a solo founder that means a complete business listing where you're eligible for one, honest reviews on the sites your buyers check, the same name, description and contact details everywhere, and an author page that shows first-hand experience. AI assistants rely on the same evidence when they decide whom to name.
:::

## What you'll be able to do

- Decide whether your business can have a Google Business Profile, and set it up if so
- Ask for reviews in a way that follows Google's and the FTC's rules
- Keep your business details consistent across the web
- Write an author or about page that shows real experience

## Why this matters

Large companies get trust for free: everyone already knows them. A one-person company has to show it. The good news is that trust signals are cheap. A listing is free, reviews come from customers you already have, and an about page takes an afternoon. They also compound: the same profile and reviews help you in Maps, in normal search, and in AI answers that recommend a business.

## Google Business Profile: who can have one

[Google Business Profile](https://www.google.com/business/) is your listing in Google Maps and local results. Google only allows it for businesses that meet customers in person: at your location, or by travelling to them.

| Your business | Eligible? | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Shop, studio or office customers visit | Yes | List your address and hours |
| You travel to customers, such as a mobile groomer | Yes, as a service-area business | Hide your home address; list the areas you serve |
| Online only, such as software or a course | No | Skip it. Put your effort into reviews on the platforms your buyers use and into Lesson 11's mentions |

If you're eligible, fill in everything: the right primary category, services, hours, photos of real work, and a description in your own words. Add **Bing Places**, which can import from Google.

## Reviews, the honest way

Ask every happy customer, at the moment they're happiest: right after a job or a result. Give them a direct link to the review form. Then reply to every review, good or bad, in a calm, specific voice.

Two sets of rules apply:

- **Google's review policy** bars offering anything in exchange for reviews and bars "review gating": asking only happy customers, or filtering people before sending them to Google.
- **The US Federal Trade Commission's rule on fake reviews**, in force since October 2024, bans buying or selling fake reviews, reviews by people who aren't customers, and undisclosed reviews by employees or family.

Ask your marketing agent to draft the request, and send it yourself:

```text
Draft a short review request to a customer who just [result]. Thank them
for one specific thing. Ask for an honest review and include this link:
[review link]. No incentives, no "only if you're happy" wording.
Under 60 words.
```

## Consistency everywhere

Search engines and assistants connect mentions of you by matching details. Pick one version and use it everywhere:

- **Name** exactly as you want it shown
- **One-line description** of what you do and for whom
- **Website, email and phone** (and address, if you show one)
- **Founder name and title**

Ask an agent to check: "Search for [business name] and list every profile or directory page you find, with the name, description and contact details each shows. Flag any that differ from these: [your details]."

## The about and author page

An author page proves experience. It's linked from every article you write and marked up as a Person (Lesson 3). It should answer:

1. **Who you are**, with a real photo
2. **What you've actually done** in this field: years, numbers, clients or results you can stand behind
3. **Why you started this business**
4. **How to check you**: links to your LinkedIn, X or other official profiles
5. **How to reach you**

:::warning
Don't invent credentials, client counts or results, even in a draft. If an AI writes "trusted by hundreds of businesses", delete it unless you can prove it. A false claim on your about page undoes every other trust signal, and in many places it's also unlawful advertising.
:::

:::try-it
Search your business name in Google, ChatGPT and Perplexity. Does each one describe you correctly? Write down every wrong or outdated detail; each one points to a profile you need to fix.
:::

:::checkpoint
Q: You run an online-only course business. Should you create a Google Business Profile?
- [ ] Yes, with your home address
- [x] No. Profiles are for businesses that meet customers in person; put the effort into reviews and mentions instead
- [ ] Yes, with a virtual office address
Why: Google limits profiles to businesses with in-person contact at a location or in a service area. Online-only businesses aren't eligible.
:::

:::checkpoint
Q: Which review request follows Google's policy?
- [ ] "If you loved it, leave us 5 stars! Not happy? Reply to this email instead."
- [ ] "Leave a review and get 10% off your next order."
- [x] "Thanks for working with us. We'd value an honest review: [link]."
Why: Google bars incentives and review gating, and the FTC bans buying reviews. Ask everyone, for an honest review, with no reward.
:::

:::takeaways
- Trust signals tell search engines and assistants a real, qualified person stands behind your site.
- Set up Google Business Profile only if you meet customers in person or in a service area.
- Ask every customer for an honest review, with no incentive and no gating.
- Keep your name and details identical everywhere, and publish an author page with checkable experience.
:::
