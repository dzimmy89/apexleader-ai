# Lesson 4: Name It and Check It

:::key-concept
A good business name is easy to say, easy to spell, hints at what you do, and isn't already taken by someone in your space. AI is great at generating long lists of names. Your job is to filter hard and run four checks: domain, social handles, business-name registries and trademarks, before you print anything.
:::

## What you'll be able to do

- Write a naming brief and get a useful long list from AI
- Filter names with a simple scorecard and real people
- Check domain and social handle availability
- Run a basic trademark and business-name search, and know when to call a lawyer

## Why this matters

Renaming after launch is expensive: new domain, new logo, new signage, lost search ranking, confused customers. Worse is getting a letter from a company that owns the trademark. Twenty minutes of checking now prevents that. For a solo founder, a clear, descriptive name also does marketing work for you, because people understand what you do before they click.

## Write a naming brief

Names come out better when the AI knows your offer, customer and constraints. Pull from your Validated Offer Doc:

```text
Help me name a new business.
Offer: [paste one-sentence offer from Exercise 3].
Customer: [who], who talk about the problem like this: [two short quotes from interviews].
Tone: [e.g. friendly, trustworthy, a little playful, not cutesy].
Constraints: 1 to 3 words, easy to spell after hearing it once, works as a .com or a short .com variant, no made-up spellings, no words that limit me if I expand beyond [current niche].
Give me 30 names in five styles: descriptive, benefit-led, metaphor, founder-name style, and invented-but-pronounceable.
For each, one line on why it fits.
```

Then push for more in the direction you like: "More like number 7 and 12, less like the invented ones."

## Filter with a scorecard

Cut the list to about eight with a quick gut pass, then score each from 1 to 5:

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Say it | Can someone spell it after hearing it once on the phone? |
| Clear | Does it hint at what you do or who it's for? |
| Distinct | Is it different from competitors in your research table? |
| Room to grow | Will it still fit if you add a service or a new customer type? |
| Feels right | Would your interviewees be comfortable recommending it? |

Then test the top three with real people. Send them to two or three of your interviewees: "Which of these would you trust with your books, and why?" Their answer beats any AI score.

## Check 1: Domain

Search the exact name as a .com at any domain registrar. If it's taken:

- Try a short, natural variant: add "hq," "co," or your niche ("groomerbooks.co" or "getgroomerbooks.com").
- Avoid hyphens and numbers. People forget them.
- Look at the site on the taken domain. If it's an active business in your field, drop the name entirely.

You can see who registered a domain and when with ICANN's lookup tool, though many owners hide their details. Buy your chosen domain as soon as the name passes all four checks. Domains are cheap; losing the one you wanted is not.

## Check 2: Social handles

Check the platforms your customers actually use (for groomers, often Instagram and Facebook). You want the same handle everywhere, or a close variant. A name with a free domain but a well-known competitor on the matching handle is a warning sign.

## Check 3: Business-name registries

In the US, your state's Secretary of State (or similar office) has a free business entity search. If an identical or very similar name is registered in your state, you may not be able to register yours as a company name there. Outside the US, check your national company register.

Note: registering a company name with your state does **not** give you trademark rights, and a free state name doesn't mean the name is free to use as a brand.

## Check 4: Trademarks

In the US, search the USPTO's free Trademark Search system at [tmsearch.uspto.gov](https://tmsearch.uspto.gov/). Search for:

- The exact name.
- Sound-alikes and spelling variants ("Groomer Books," "Groomr Books," "Groomer Bookkeeping").
- The most distinctive word on its own.

Focus on live marks. The USPTO's question is "likelihood of confusion": does a mark look or sound similar, mean something similar, and cover related goods or services? Related doesn't mean identical. Services sold to the same buyers or advertised together can conflict. The USPTO also warns that narrowing your search by class is risky because you can miss related marks filed in another class. If you sell outside the US, the WIPO Global Brand Database searches many countries' trademark records.

Then search the web and app stores for the name plus your category. The USPTO notes that someone with common law rights, from simply using a name in commerce, can act against you even if they never registered it.

Use AI to widen the search, not to make the call:

```text
I want to name my business "[name]" for [offer]. List 15 spelling and sound-alike variants I should search in a trademark database and on Google.
Then list the trademark classes that probably cover [my services], with a one-line reason for each, and remind me what to confirm with a trademark attorney.
```

This is general information, not legal advice. If you plan to invest in branding, file your own trademark, or find anything close, book a short consult with a trademark attorney. It costs far less than a rebrand. The USPTO also points to lower-cost help such as law school clinics.

:::try-it
Take one name you like and spend five minutes: check the .com, the Instagram handle, and run the exact name in the USPTO Trademark Search. Write down what you find. Most founders learn their favorite is taken.
:::

:::warning
Don't buy signage, print materials or ads until the name passes all four checks. Don't rely on an AI assistant's statement that a name is "available" or "not trademarked." It can't see current registries reliably; search them yourself.
:::

:::checkpoint
Q: Your favorite name is free as a .com, and your state registry shows no match, but an active company in a related service uses a nearly identical name. What should you do?
- [ ] Use it; the domain and state registration mean it's yours
- [ ] Ask an AI assistant whether it's legally safe and go with its answer
- [x] Treat it as high risk: pick another name or get a trademark attorney's opinion before using it
Why: A free domain and state registration don't give trademark rights. A similar name in a related field is the classic confusion risk.
:::

:::takeaways
- Give AI a real naming brief from your offer and interview quotes, then filter hard.
- Test your top three names with real customers.
- Run four checks: domain, social handles, business-name registry and trademark search.
- AI widens the search; registries and, when needed, a lawyer make the call.
:::
