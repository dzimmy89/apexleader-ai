# Lesson 3: Shape the Offer and the Price

:::key-concept
An offer is a specific promise to a specific customer: what they get, what changes for them, what it costs and what happens next. Build it from your interview evidence, price it against the customer's alternatives and the value of the outcome, then test it by asking for a real commitment before you build.
:::

## What you'll be able to do

- Write a one-paragraph offer built from your interview evidence
- Set a price using three anchors: alternatives, value and your own costs
- Design simple tiers without overcomplicating your first product
- Test the offer with a pre-sale or a letter of intent before building

## Why this matters

Founders tend to underprice and overbuild. You spend weeks on features nobody asked for, then charge too little to stay in business. A clear offer fixes both. It narrows what you build to what customers said they need, and it makes the price feel obvious because it's tied to an outcome they care about. AI is a good sparring partner here: it can draft options fast and argue the customer's side. The decision stays yours.

## Build the offer from evidence

Go back to your interview synthesis. Your offer should use the customer's own words for the pain and the outcome. Use this structure:

| Part | Groomer Books example |
|---|---|
| Who it's for | Independent grooming shops with 1 to 5 staff |
| The problem, in their words | "I do my books at midnight once a quarter and dread tax season" |
| The outcome | Books done every month, tax-ready, without touching a spreadsheet |
| What they get | Monthly reconciliation, a one-page monthly report, a year-end package for their accountant |
| What they do | Connect bank and booking app once, answer a few questions a month |
| What it's not | Not tax filing, not payroll |
| Proof or risk reversal | First month free if the books aren't done by the 10th |

Ask AI to draft from your actual notes, not from its imagination:

```text
Here is my interview synthesis: [paste].
Draft three versions of a one-paragraph offer for this customer.
Each must: use at least one phrase customers actually said, name a concrete outcome, say what's included and what isn't.
Version A: simplest possible. Version B: premium. Version C: one I probably haven't considered.
```

Pick one. Cut anything customers didn't ask for.

## Price with three anchors

There's no perfect formula, but three anchors keep you honest.

1. **Alternatives.** What does the customer use now, and what does it cost? From your research and interviews: software subscriptions, a general bookkeeper, their own evenings. Your price sits in relation to these.
2. **Value.** What's the outcome worth to them? Hours saved times what their time is worth, fees or penalties avoided, stress gone before tax season. Price as a fraction of that value.
3. **Your floor.** Your hours, tools and costs per customer, plus a margin. If the price is below this, the offer doesn't work no matter how much they want it.

```text
Help me price this offer: [paste offer].
Alternatives and their costs: [paste from your research table].
What interviewees said about time and money spent: [paste].
My delivery time per customer per month: about [X] hours; tools cost about [Y] per customer.
Give me a floor price, a value-based ceiling and a recommended launch price.
Show your reasoning step by step and list which inputs are weakest.
Then argue against your recommended price as a skeptical customer would.
```

Watch for the AI's bias toward cheap prices. If your interviews showed people already pay a bookkeeper, you can usually charge in that range or above for a specialist.

Tax treatment of what you sell (sales tax, VAT) varies by place and product. That's general information; check with an accountant before you publish prices.

## Keep tiers simple

Three tiers is a common pattern because many buyers pick the middle one. For a first offer, two or three is plenty:

- **Starter:** the core outcome, nothing extra.
- **Standard:** core plus the most-requested extra from interviews. This is the one you want most people to buy.
- **Cleanup or premium:** a one-time or higher-touch option for people who are behind or want more.

Every tier should map to something customers asked for. If you can't point to an interview quote for a feature, leave it out.

## Test it before you build

Interviews tell you about the problem. Only a real ask tells you about the offer. Pick one test:

- **Pre-sale.** Offer the first 5 founding customers a discount for paying now, with a full refund if you don't deliver by a date.
- **Letter of intent.** For bigger B2B deals, a short signed note that says they intend to buy at a stated price when it's ready.
- **Waitlist with a deposit.** A small refundable deposit beats an email address.
- **Concierge pilot.** Deliver the outcome by hand for one or two customers before you automate anything.

Go back to the most interested people from your interviews first. Let AI draft the message, then you edit and send it yourself:

```text
Write a short follow-up email to a groomer I interviewed last week.
Thank her, remind her of the problem she described ([quote]).
Offer to be one of 5 founding customers: [offer], [price], founding discount [X], full refund if not delivered by [date].
One clear call to action: reply "in" or book a 15-minute call. No pressure language.
```

Set a pass bar before you send, for example: "At least 3 of 10 say yes with money or a signed letter." Write it down so you can't move the goalposts later.

:::try-it
Write your offer in one sentence: "For [who], who [problem in their words], I [outcome] for [price], unlike [main alternative]." Then ask an AI chat to play a skeptical customer and give you their three toughest objections.
:::

:::warning
Taking money before you deliver is a promise. Use a payment link you control, state the delivery date and refund terms clearly, and keep the founding-customer list small enough that you can deliver by hand. Never let an AI agent send offers or take payments on your behalf without your review.
:::

:::checkpoint
Q: Ten interviewees loved your offer. You asked for a small refundable deposit and nobody paid. What does that tell you?
- [ ] The offer is validated; the deposit was just bad timing
- [x] The offer, price or audience isn't right yet; dig into why before you build
- [ ] Drop the price to zero and try again
Why: Commitments are the real test. Praise without payment usually means the problem isn't urgent enough or the offer doesn't fit yet.
:::

:::takeaways
- Build the offer from interview evidence, using the customer's own words.
- Price with three anchors: alternatives, value and your cost floor.
- Keep tiers simple and tie every feature to something customers asked for.
- Validate the offer with a real commitment, against a pass bar you set in advance.
:::
