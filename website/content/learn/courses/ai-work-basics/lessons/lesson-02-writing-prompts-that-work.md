# Lesson 2: Writing Prompts That Work

:::key-concept
A good prompt gives the assistant what a new employee would need: the role, the goal, the material, the format you want back, and an example. Most bad answers come from missing context, not from a bad tool.
:::

## What you'll be able to do

- Write a prompt with the five parts: role, goal, material, format, example.
- Turn a vague request into a specific one.
- Save a prompt you can reuse every week.

## Why this matters

The difference between "write a follow-up email" and a five-part prompt is the difference between a draft you throw away and one you send with two edits.

## The five parts

1. **Role**: "You are the office manager of a three-person accounting firm."
2. **Goal**: "Write a friendly reminder to clients who have not sent their documents."
3. **Material**: paste the list of missing documents and the deadline.
4. **Format**: "Under 120 words, a subject line, no exclamation marks."
5. **Example**: paste one past email you liked.

You will not always need all five, but when an answer misses, check which part you left out.

## Ask for questions first

For bigger jobs, end your prompt with "Before you start, ask me up to three questions you need answered." The assistant will often catch the context you forgot.

:::try-it
Take a request you typed into an assistant this week. Rewrite it with all five parts and compare the two answers.
:::

## Save what works

When a prompt gives a good result, save it in a shared document with a name like "Weekly client reminder". Next week you paste it, swap the material, and you are done in minutes.

:::takeaways
- Give role, goal, material, format and an example.
- Let the assistant ask you questions on bigger jobs.
- Keep a library of prompts that work.
:::
